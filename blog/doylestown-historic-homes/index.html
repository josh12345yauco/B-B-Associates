Doylestown Kitchen Remodeling: What to Expect in a Historic Home

December 2024 · 6 min read
Home › Journal › Doylestown Kitchen Remodeling: What to Expect in a Historic Home

Doylestown and the surrounding Bucks County communities are home to some of the most beautiful older properties in the Philadelphia region. Federal-style colonials, Victorian farmhouses, and mid-century homes along the Delaware River all have something in common: hidden complexity. Renovating a kitchen in one of these homes is deeply rewarding — but only with a contractor who knows what to look for before the first wall comes down. This is precisely the kind of work we specialize in across Doylestown and New Hope.

What We Discover Before We Swing a Hammer

Before any demo begins in a historic home, B&B's team conducts a thorough pre-construction assessment. Here's what we look for:

  • Plaster walls and lath: Older homes often have plaster over lath instead of drywall. Demo costs more and takes longer — but it also reveals beautiful original framing.
  • Knob-and-tube or aluminum wiring: Homes built before 1960 frequently have electrical systems that need partial or full replacement before a modern kitchen renovation can proceed. We build this into every estimate.
  • Load-bearing walls: The open-concept kitchen trend requires structural analysis in any home over 40 years old. We always pull permits and work with licensed engineers.
  • Asbestos and lead: Tiles, joint compound, and paint in homes built before 1980 may contain hazardous materials. B&B handles all required testing and abatement as part of our standard pre-construction process.

Why Historic Bucks County Homes Are Different

Bucks County's housing stock is unusually old by American standards — it is not uncommon to work in homes that predate the Revolution. That history brings construction methods you simply don't find in newer builds:

  • Fieldstone foundations that affect where you can run new plumbing and how you support an island.
  • Hand-hewn timber framing rather than dimensional lumber, which changes how structural work is engineered.
  • Wide-plank floors and exposed beams that are irreplaceable and deserve to be designed around, not covered over.
  • Out-of-square rooms, where almost nothing is plumb or level — a reality that makes custom cabinetry, scribed to fit, far superior to stock boxes.

Our Historic 1700s Farmhouse Kitchen is the clearest example of this philosophy in action: we preserved the original pine plank floors and exposed beams while quietly introducing modern insulation, structural safety, and a fully functional layout. For a deeper discussion of these conditions, see our companion guide on what to expect with older Bucks County homes.

Preserving Character While Upgrading Function

The best historic kitchen renovations honor what makes the home special. We've learned to balance period-appropriate design choices — beadboard cabinets, apron-front sinks, brass hardware — with modern performance: induction cooktops, integrated refrigerators, and quartz surfaces that look like soapstone.

The goal is a kitchen that feels like it has always belonged in the house, yet performs like it was built yesterday. That balance is an art. Push too far toward preservation and you get a beautiful room that frustrates a modern cook; push too far toward modern and you erase the soul of the home. Getting it right requires a team that designs and builds under one roof, so the people choosing the soapstone-look quartz are the same people fitting it against a 250-year-old wall.

Permits, Townships, and Historic Districts

Doylestown Borough, Doylestown Township, New Hope, and Solebury each have their own permit processes, and some properties sit within historic overlay districts that govern exterior changes. While interior kitchen work usually isn't restricted, any change involving windows, additions, or exterior walls may require review. B&B handles all permitting and inspection coordination with the relevant municipality as part of our standard scope — you won't be standing in line at the township office. Our Bucks County service page outlines the communities we cover.

Budgeting and Timeline for a Historic Kitchen

Historic homes carry more unknowns, so realistic budgeting matters. Beyond the visible scope, plan for systems upgrades — $8,000 to $20,000 is common for electrical and plumbing in a pre-1960 home — plus a healthy contingency for what we find once walls are open. Timelines also run longer than new-construction kitchens; plaster demo, structural reinforcement, and custom-fit cabinetry all add days. Our kitchen renovation timeline guide explains how the phases stack up, and our custom cabinetry guide covers why scribed, made-to-measure cabinetry is the right call in an out-of-square historic room.

Designing Around Original Features

The features that make a historic Bucks County home special — exposed beams, deep window sills, original hardware, wide-plank floors — are exactly the elements a thoughtful renovation should celebrate rather than hide. In practice, that means designing the new kitchen to the house, not imposing a generic layout onto it. We routinely scribe custom cabinetry to follow an out-of-plumb wall, set cabinet heights to respect an existing beam, and choose finishes that complement rather than compete with original wood. This is precisely why stock cabinetry struggles in these homes and why our custom cabinetry approach matters so much in older properties.

Bringing Modern Systems Into an Old House

A historic kitchen has to perform like a modern one. That invisible work — behind the plaster and under the floor — is where much of the value lives:

  • Insulation and air sealing: Many pre-war homes have little to none. A renovation is the moment to add it, dramatically improving comfort and efficiency without altering the look.
  • Electrical modernization: Replacing knob-and-tube, adding grounded circuits, and sizing the panel for today's appliances.
  • Plumbing and venting: Updating aging supply lines and ensuring proper drain venting for a relocated sink or new island.
  • Heating and cooling: Integrating modern comfort into rooms that were never designed for it, often with low-profile or concealed solutions.

Our 1700s farmhouse kitchen is the definitive example: original pine floors and beams preserved on the surface, fully modern systems and structural safety underneath.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

We're often called in to correct work that went wrong. The recurring mistakes in historic homes are avoidable:

  • Skipping the structural assessment. Opening a wall without confirming whether it's load-bearing is dangerous and expensive to fix.
  • Forcing a perfectly modern, hard-edged design into a home with soft, settled lines — the contrast can feel jarring rather than intentional.
  • Underestimating the budget and timeline. Older homes carry more unknowns; a realistic contingency prevents mid-project stress.
  • Removing irreplaceable original material that could have been restored. Once a hand-hewn beam or original floor is gone, it's gone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you remodel a historic kitchen without losing its character? Absolutely — that balance is our specialty. We preserve and design around original features like beams, plank floors, and period hardware while quietly modernizing systems and function. Our 1700s farmhouse kitchen is the clearest example.

Why Local Experience Matters in an Old House

There is no substitute for having worked in homes like yours, in the towns where you live. A contractor who has only ever renovated newer construction will be genuinely surprised by what a 1850s Doylestown farmhouse hides behind its walls — and surprises during construction are expensive. Our 13 years across Bucks County mean we have already seen the fieldstone that complicates an island, the hand-hewn beam that can't simply be cut, the layers of flooring added by four previous owners, and the wiring that no longer meets code. We price for those realities instead of pretending they don't exist.

That experience also shapes design. We know which period details are worth preserving and which can be reimagined, how to source reclaimed or period-appropriate materials when a project calls for them, and how to make a brand-new kitchen feel as though it has always belonged in a centuries-old home. The result is a renovation that respects the house's history while finally making it work for the way you actually cook and gather. It's the difference between a kitchen that looks "renovated" and one that looks like it was always meant to be there.
